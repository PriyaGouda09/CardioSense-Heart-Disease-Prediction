ABSTRACT

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting
for nearly one-third of global deaths annually. While existing machine learning models
often formulate the problem as binary classification—disease present or absent—clinical
practice requires granular severity staging to guide treatment decisions. This study
addresses the multiclass cardiovascular disease severity prediction challenge using
the publicly available UCI Heart Disease Dataset. We applied a complete preprocessing
and feature engineering pipeline, developed five clinically motivated engineered
features, and trained three baseline classifiers (Support Vector Machine, Random
Forest, and Multilayer Perceptron) with systematic hyperparameter tuning via
GridSearchCV. Our best model, a neural network architecture with a (128, 64)
hidden layer structure, achieved 59.24% test accuracy across five severity classes.
All models demonstrated substantially lower performance on the two most severe disease
classes, reflecting the combined effects of clinical feature overlap and severe class
underrepresentation (only 28 instances of Class 4 in our 920-patient dataset).
These findings establish a rigorous empirical baseline that quantifies the specific
performance gaps motivating hierarchical and ensemble-based approaches for clinical
deployment.

Keywords—cardiovascular disease, multiclass classification, feature engineering,
support vector machine, random forest, multilayer perceptron, class imbalance,
UCI heart disease dataset

I. INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) caused 19.8 million deaths in 2022 according to World
Health Organization estimates. Beyond mortality burden, these conditions generate
enormous healthcare expenditures and reduced quality of life for survivors. Most
patients recover differently after cardiac events, and clinicians face the challenge
of determining which individuals need aggressive intervention versus conservative
management. Existing risk prediction systems often reduce this complex clinical
reality to a single binary outcome: disease present or absent. Yet this binary
formulation discards critical information—a completely healthy patient requires
different clinical management than one with sustained ventricular dysfunction.

The severity staging of CVD typically follows five levels: Stage 0 (no disease),
Stage 1 (mild disease with minimal symptoms), Stage 2 (moderate disease),
Stage 3 (advanced disease with functional limitation), and Stage 4 (very severe
or end-stage disease). Predictions at this granular level would enable earlier
intervention for Stage 1 patients and more intensive treatment allocation for
progressive stages. Yet published machine learning studies routinely report binary
classification accuracies exceeding 90% while multiclass models on the same datasets
typically achieve 55–68% accuracy. This "granularity gap" stems from two distinct
factors: first, adjacent severity stages often share overlapping clinical markers
(a patient with moderate chest pain resembles one with mild chest pain more than
one in cardiogenic shock), and second, most real-world heart disease datasets
exhibit extreme class imbalance where mild and asymptomatic cases dominate while
severe cases remain rare.

Our contribution focuses on establishing a reproducible baseline for multiclass
severity prediction using a standardised dataset and rigorous experimental methodology.
Specifically, we present: (1) a complete data preprocessing pipeline tailored to
the nuances of cardiac datasets, including median imputation for skewed continuous
measurements; (2) five clinically interpretable engineered features grounded in
cardiological knowledge; (3) systematic hyperparameter optimisation across three
architecturally distinct classifiers—SVM, Random Forest, and MLP—using stratified
cross-validation; and (4) detailed per-class performance analysis that identifies
exactly where these architectures fail. By quantifying this baseline, we establish
a foundation for future hierarchical approaches that align with clinical decision
workflows.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II surveys related work
on binary versus multiclass CVD prediction, advanced architectures narrowing the
accuracy gap, and the role of feature engineering in model performance. Section III
describes the UCI Heart Disease Dataset and our preprocessing steps. Section IV details
the five engineered features and their clinical motivation. Section V outlines our
methodology, including encoding and scaling, model selection rationale, and
hyperparameter tuning strategy. Section VI presents results with per-class analysis.
Section VII discusses limitations and implications. Section VIII concludes with
specific directions for future work.


II. RELATED WORK

The Accuracy Gap Between Binary and Multiclass Prediction

The disparity in model performance between binary and multiclass CVD prediction
is well established across the literature. Amirthaa et al. reported stacked ensemble
methods (combining Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting in a meta-learning
framework) that achieved 90.2% binary classification accuracy but only 64.1% multiclass
accuracy on the same UCI dataset. Critically, their approach completely failed on
the most severe class, recording zero precision and zero recall for Class 4 patients.
Jackson et al. demonstrated this pattern again: Random Forest achieved 57.1% multiclass
accuracy post-SMOTE oversampling, substantially below the 90%+ rates typical for
binary formulations. Lakshmi and Devi reported similar ranges across classical
architectures (Support Vector Machine 68%, Decision Tree 59%, Gradient Boosting 63%),
confirming that the gap persists across kernel-based, tree-based, and distance-based
methods. This consistency suggests the gap reflects inherent dataset structure rather
than algorithm selection.

Advanced Architectures and the Path Forward

Some studies have narrowed the multiclass gap through richer data integration and
deeper learning models. Ushasri and Ketepalli's IoMT-integrated MLP achieved 91.3%
multiclass accuracy on UCI data, substantially outperforming the 55–68% baseline
reported in simpler studies. Their approach augmented static UCI features with
real-time IoMT (Internet of Medical Things) sensor data, capturing temporal dynamics
absent in cross-sectional datasets. Similarly, Magar and Kaur explored hybrid deep
learning architectures (CNN+LSTM) reaching 95.6% multiclass accuracy, though their
evaluation domain differed from the standard UCI benchmark. These results suggest
two pathways: temporal information integration improves discriminative power, and
end-to-end deep learning with automatic feature extraction can identify patterns
hidden in engineered features. However, both approaches require either richer data
infrastructure (IoMT sensors) or larger training corpora—resources many clinical
centres lack. This motivates the current work: establishing a strong baseline
on publicly available data before pursuing more complex variants.

Feature Engineering and the Role of Domain Knowledge

The literature emphasises that preprocessing and feature engineering substantially
impact classifier performance on CVD datasets. Lakshmi and Devi demonstrated that
IQR-based outlier detection followed by clipping improved model accuracy across
all three classifiers they tested. Jackson et al. examined the impact of automated
feature selection methods (SelectKBest, Recursive Feature Elimination) and reported
a counterintuitive finding: while SMOTE oversampling raised Random Forest multiclass
accuracy from 45.5% to 57.1%, applying feature selection methods actually degraded
performance. This suggests that tree-based methods benefit from seeing the full
feature space (allowing the algorithm to discover feature interactions) rather than
pre-selected subsets. This observation motivated our approach: rather than applying
automated feature selection, we engineered domain-driven features grounded in clinical
knowledge about cardiac pathophysiology. We hypothesised that explicit markers—such
as age groups aligned with cardiological risk brackets, cholesterol thresholds based
on standard clinical guidelines, and interaction terms capturing the combined effect
of age and cardiac capacity—would provide higher discriminative signal than either
raw or automatically selected features.


III. DATASET AND PREPROCESSING

Dataset Description

The UCI Heart Disease Dataset aggregates 920 patient records from four hospital studies
(Cleveland, Hungary, Switzerland, and Virginia) conducted across the 1980s–1990s. Each
record contains 16 clinical and demographic attributes including age (years), blood
pressure (resting systolic, mmHg), serum cholesterol (mg/dL), maximum heart rate achieved
during testing (beats/minute), ST depression from exercise (oldpeak, mm), and categorical
features such as chest pain type, presence of diabetes, resting electrocardiogram type,
exercise-induced angina, and three ECG slope and thallium scintigraphy variables. The
target variable, num, grades heart disease severity into five classes: Class 0 (n=411,
no disease), Class 1 (n=265, mild disease), Class 2 (n=109, moderate disease), Class 3
(n=107, advanced disease), and Class 4 (n=28, very severe disease). This distribution
reveals pronounced class imbalance—the healthy and mild-disease classes collectively
represent 73.6% of observations, while severe classes comprise less than 15%.

Preprocessing Pipeline

Our preprocessing followed a clinically informed sequence designed to handle missing
values according to data type and distribution properties. Initial exploration revealed
three features with substantial missingness: slope (309 missing, 33.6%), ca (611 missing,
66.4%), and thal (486 missing, 52.8%). For the six numerical features (age, trestbps,
chol, thalch, oldpeak, ca), we imputed missing values with the per-column median. The
median is robust to outliers—particularly important for cardiac measurements, which
often exhibit right-skewed distributions due to clinical measurement ceilings. Categorical
features (sex, cp, fbs, restecg, exang, slope, thal) were imputed with each column's mode,
the most frequently occurring category. Following imputation, we removed 43 duplicate rows,
resulting in 877 unique records.

Outlier management followed the interquartile range (IQR) clipping method. For each
numerical feature, we calculated the first quartile (Q1) and third quartile (Q3), derived
the IQR as Q3 − Q1, and defined outlier thresholds as:
  Lower bound = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR
  Upper bound = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

Rather than removing outliers (which risks losing rare but clinically important cases),
we clipped values to these bounds. This approach preserves sample size while reducing
the influence of extreme measurement errors or transcription mistakes. IQR clipping is
particularly appropriate for medical data where extreme outliers often indicate data
quality problems rather than genuine patient heterogeneity.


IV. FEATURE ENGINEERING

Engineered features address a fundamental observation from exploratory data analysis:
raw features showed substantial overlap across adjacent severity classes, suggesting
that simple threshold-based rules would misclassify many patients. Additionally,
univariate analysis revealed that interaction between features—rather than individual
feature values—sometimes indicated clinical severity. We engineered five features
grounded in cardiological domain knowledge:

1. Age_Group (categorical ordinal)
Age is a cornerstone risk factor for cardiovascular disease, yet its relationship
with severity is non-linear. Rather than treating age as a continuous variable, we
binned it into clinically meaningful brackets aligned with standard risk stratification:
age < 40 (low baseline risk, Group 0), age 40–50 (emerging risk period, Group 1),
age 50–60 (accelerated risk, Group 2), and age ≥ 60 (high-risk elderly, Group 3).
This captures the stepped increase in CVD prevalence across decades without assuming
a linear progression.

2. Chol_Risk (binary flag)
Serum cholesterol exceeding 200 mg/dL is the standard clinical threshold for elevated
cardiovascular risk used globally by cardiologists. We created a binary feature where
chol_risk = 1 if cholesterol > 200 mg/dL, else 0. This transforms a continuous
measurement into a clinically actionable risk marker.

3. High_Thalch (binary flag)
Maximum heart rate achieved during stress testing (thalch) inversely correlates with
disease severity and reflects overall cardiovascular fitness. Patients above the dataset
median thalch (approximately 153 beats/minute) possess better exercise tolerance and lower
disease severity on average. This feature captures that distinction: high_thalch = 1 if
thalch > mean(thalch), else 0.

4. Age_Thalch_Ratio (numerical interaction)
A patient's age combined with their maximum achievable heart rate tells a more complete
story than either variable alone. An older patient with low cardiac capacity faces higher
risk than a younger one with identical cardiac parameters. We derived the ratio age /
(thalch + 1), adding 1 to the denominator to avoid division by zero and to dampen the
effect of measurements where thalch is extremely low. Our subsequent analysis showed this
interaction term emerged as the top-ranked feature in Random Forest, validating that the
age-fitness relationship indeed carries more predictive signal than raw age data.

5. Oldpeak_Cat (categorical ordinal)
ST depression during exercise (oldpeak, measured in millimeters on an electrocardiogram)
is a precise indicator of myocardial ischaemia severity. Rather than treating this as a
continuous variable, we categorised it according to clinical interpretation guidelines:
oldpeak 0 mm (none), 0–1 mm (mild ischaemia), 1–2 mm (moderate ischaemia), > 2 mm
(severe ischaemia). This aligns the feature with how cardiologists actually interpret
these measurements.

V. METHODOLOGY

Encoding and Scaling

All categorical and ordinal features (age_group, chol_risk, high_thalch, oldpeak_cat,
plus the original categorical features: sex, cp, fbs, restecg, exang, slope, thal) were
label-encoded into integer representations using scikit-learn's LabelEncoder. Label
encoding is appropriate here because our models (SVM, MLP) treat integer inputs as
ordinal—no one-hot encoding was necessary.

All features, both encoded categorical and raw numerical, were then standardised to
zero mean and unit variance using StandardScaler. Standardisation is mathematically
critical for SVM (which minimises a distance-based objective function) and for MLP
(where gradient descent optimisation proceeds more stably when all input dimensions
have comparable magnitudes). Random Forest, being a tree-based ensemble, is invariant
to feature scaling; we applied scaling uniformly to maintain consistency.

We partitioned the data into training and test sets at an 80/20 ratio,
stratifying by target class to preserve class proportions in both subsets. This stratified
split is essential when handling imbalanced multiclass problems—random splitting risks
excluding rare classes entirely from the training set, making it impossible to learn
their decision boundaries.

Model Selection Rationale

Support Vector Machine (SVM) with kernel methods: SVMs are consistently reported across
multiclass CVD studies as strong performers. The kernel trick allows non-linear decision
boundaries in high-dimensional spaces without explicitly computing transformations,
making SVMs particularly effective when features are not linearly separable.

Random Forest: Tree ensembles combine high empirical performance with interpretability—
specifically, they generate feature importance scores showing which features drive
predictions. For clinicians, understanding which patient factors matter most is as
important as prediction accuracy itself. Random Forests also handle categorical features
naturally and are robust to outliers.

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP): Neural networks capture non-linear feature interactions
through multiple layers of hidden neurons, and their end-to-end training strategy can
discover patterns missed by hand-engineered features or fixed-kernel methods. We chose
MLP as a bridge to deeper architectures; success with MLP motivates future exploration
of convolutional or recurrent layers on richer datasets.

Hyperparameter Tuning Strategy

We employed GridSearchCV with 5-fold StratifiedKFold cross-validation. Stratified
k-fold is mandatory here: with only 28 Class 4 samples across 877 records, random
k-fold splits risk assigning all Class 4 instances to one fold, leaving other folds
without enough Class 4 data to compute meaningful classwise metrics. Stratification
ensures each fold receives a representative class distribution.

Grid configurations for each model:

SVM (12 combinations):
  C ∈ {0.1, 1, 10} (regularisation strength)
  kernel ∈ {'rbf', 'linear'} (Radial Basis Function for non-linear, linear for
           linear decision boundaries)
  gamma ∈ {'scale', 'auto'} (kernel coefficient; applies only to RBF kernel)

Random Forest (12 combinations):
  n_estimators ∈ {100, 200} (number of trees)
  max_depth ∈ {None, 10, 20} (tree depth limit; None allows unlimited depth)
  min_samples_split ∈ {2, 5} (minimum samples required to split a node)

MLP (16 combinations):
  hidden_layer_sizes ∈ {(64, 32), (128, 64)} (two hidden layer architectures)
  activation ∈ {'relu', 'tanh'} (neuron activation functions)
  alpha ∈ {0.0001, 0.001} (L2 regularisation parameter)
  max_iter = 500 (training iteration limit)
  early_stopping = True (stop training if validation loss plateaus)

The evaluation metric was accuracy on the validation folds; the best-performing
hyperparameters from cross-validation were then applied to the held-out test set.

The MLP achieved the highest test accuracy at 59.24%, representing a 2.72 percentage
point improvement over SVM and a 2.72 percentage point improvement over Random Forest.
However, the MLP's cross-validation accuracy (55.31%) substantially lags its test
accuracy (59.24%)—a 3.93 percentage point gap that suggests modest overfitting to the
particular test fold. This discrepancy warrants caution in interpreting MLP as superior,
though it may reflect random variation given the small test set (n=175).

Per-Class Performance Analysis

The aggregate accuracy figures mask critical class-specific failures. Table III presents
the full classification report for each model.

SVM Classification Report:
The SVM concentrated predictions on Class 0, achieving 88% recall (detecting 88% of
healthy patients) with 75% precision. This yielded an F1 score of 0.81. For Class 1
(mild disease), performance degraded: 57% recall and 44% precision (F1=0.50). Classes
2, 3, and 4 received zero or near-zero predictions; the SVM assigned almost all cases
to Class 0, essentially learning a degenerate solution that "predicts healthy" for
difficult cases rather than attempting to separate the minority classes. This is a known
pathology in imbalanced classification: large-margin methods like SVM can minimise
training error by concentrating on the majority class.

Random Forest Classification Report:
Random Forest distributed predictions more broadly across all five classes. Class 0
again dominated (F1=0.78), Class 1 showed modest improvement (F1=0.51), and Classes 2
and 3 garnered non-zero predictions (F1=0.12 and 0.18, respectively). Class 4 remained
completely undetected (precision and recall both 0). Feature importance analysis revealed
age_thalch_ratio as the single most important feature (importance ≈ 0.087), followed by
chol, thalch, age, and cp (chest pain type). This ranking validates our domain-driven
feature engineering: the engineered age-fitness interaction term outranked both of its
constituent variables, confirming that their combined effect matters more than their
individual contributions.

MLP Classification Report:
The MLP achieved the best F1 scores for Classes 1 (F1=0.53) and 3 (F1=0.27) among all
models. Notably, for Class 4 (very severe disease), the MLP reported 100% precision with
17% recall. This apparent paradox—identifying one Class 4 case correctly while missing
five others—arises because only a handful of Class 4 instances exist; the model made a
single correct identification, yielding perfect precision on that isolated prediction. This
serves as a stark reminder: when evaluating classifiers on severely imbalanced classes,
per-class metrics can be misleading if not interpreted with sample counts in mind.

Cross-Cutting Performance Pattern

A consistent pattern emerged across all three architecturally distinct methods: Classes 0
and 1 received robust detection while Classes 3 and 4 remained largely undetected. In our
test set: Class 0 had 82 samples, Class 1 had 53, Class 2 had 22, Class 3 had 21, and
Class 4 had 6. The severe class imbalance directly correlates with degrading per-class
accuracy. This finding aligns with Amirthaa et al.'s report of zero precision and recall
for Class 4 in their stacked ensemble, and Jackson et al.'s observation that SMOTE
oversampling actually hurt Random Forest despite raising the overall multiclass accuracy.
The implication is that shallow feature engineering and standard training procedures
cannot overcome extreme class imbalance alone. Rather, addressing this requires either
data augmentation (SMOTE, oversampling, synthetic data generation), cost-sensitive loss
functions (weighting minority class errors more heavily), or fundamentally different
architectures such as one-vs-rest binary classifiers stacked in a hierarchical tree.


VII. DISCUSSION

These baseline results quantify the exact scale of the multiclass severity prediction
challenge and identify where standard classifiers fail. The best-performing model (MLP
at 59.24% accuracy) remains well below clinically acceptable thresholds, and its collapse
on severe classes limits utility for high-risk patient identification.

Limitations and Corresponding Future Directions

First, class imbalance: our dataset contains 73.6% of all instances in Classes 0–1,
with only 28 samples of Class 4. No classifier can reliably learn from such sparse
representations. Future work should explore Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique
(SMOTE) to artificially augment minority classes, cost-sensitive learning where
misclassifying a Class 4 patient incurs higher penalty than misclassifying a Class 0
patient, or focal loss functions that down-weight easy-to-classify examples and focus
training on hard cases.

Second, flat multi-way classification architecture: treating all five classes symmetrically
ignores clinical reality. Cardiologists first determine presence or absence of disease
(binary triage), then stage severity among positive cases. A two-stage hierarchical model
where Stage 1 classifies healthy (Class 0) versus diseased (Classes 1–4), and Stage 2
classifies severity among the diseased, aligns with actual diagnostic workflows. The
binary-versus-diseased decision likely has higher accuracy than the full five-way problem,
reducing pressure on the second stage.

Third, lack of explainability: our models operate as black boxes. SHapley Additive
exPlanations (SHAP) values would decompose each prediction into per-feature contributions,
enabling cardiologists to understand why a model classified a specific patient as Class 2
rather than Class 1. For high-stakes medical applications, interpretability is not a luxury
but a requirement.

Strengths and Validating Observations

Despite these limitations, the work yields interpretable findings. The fact that
age_thalch_ratio became the top feature in Random Forest is clinically sensible: a patient's
age combined with their exercise tolerance capacity is more informative than either metric
alone. Older patients with low cardiac capacity face compounding risks. This emergent insight,
derived directly from our engineering choices, suggests that domain-informed feature creation
outperforms automated methods on this dataset—a finding consistent with Jackson et al.'s
observation that automated feature selection actually hurt tree-based multiclass performance.


VIII. CONCLUSION

We trained and evaluated three baseline classifiers—Support Vector Machine, Random Forest,
and Multilayer Perceptron—for five-class cardiovascular disease severity prediction on the
UCI Heart Disease Dataset. The MLP achieved the highest test accuracy at 59.24%, though all
models exhibited near-complete failure on the two most severe disease classes due to extreme
class imbalance. These results establish quantitative evidence that standard multiclass
approaches collapse on imbalanced medical data, motivating three specific directions for
future work:

1. Implement class-balanced training strategies: apply SMOTE oversampling for minority
   classes and cost-sensitive learning where minority misclassifications incur higher
   penalty weights.

2. Develop hierarchical classification: design a two-stage pipeline where Stage 1 performs
   binary detection (healthy vs. diseased) and Stage 2 performs severity staging among
   positive cases, aligning with clinical triage workflows.

3. Integrate explainability mechanisms: generate SHAP force plots and summary plots to
   decompose individual predictions into feature contributions, enabling clinicians to
   trust and act on model outputs.

Building trustworthy clinical AI systems requires establishing rigorous baselines that
expose failure modes, quantify performance gaps, and guide architectural innovations.
This work provides that foundation.
