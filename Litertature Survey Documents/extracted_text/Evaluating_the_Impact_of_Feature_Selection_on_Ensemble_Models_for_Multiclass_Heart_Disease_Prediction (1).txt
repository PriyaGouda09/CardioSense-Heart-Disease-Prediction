Abstract—This study discusses the impact of different feature
selection techniques on the performance of ensemble learning
algorithms, namely Random Forest and XGBoost, in multiclass
heart disease prediction. A real-world clinical dataset with class
imbalances was used, which were resolved using the Synthetic Mi-
nority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE). Three different fea-
ture selection techniques—Chi-Square, Mutual Information, and
SelectFromModel—were tested against baseline models without
feature selection. The models were trained with hyperparameter
tuning, and their performance metrics were compared using
accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The results showed that
both Random Forest and XGBoost achieved optimal performance
without feature selection, with the Random Forest model achiev-
ing the highest accuracy of 57.1%. SHAP interpretations showed
that erythrocyte, hemoglobin, and hematocrit were the major
features that determined model predictions. These results suggest
that tree-based ensemble techniques may have an inherent
ability for feature relevance handling, reducing the need for
explicit selection methods. This study adds to the knowledge and
efficiency of predictive modeling in clinical diagnostics.

I. INTRODUCTION
Cardiovascular illness is a leading cause of worldwide
mortality, reported by the World Health Organization (WHO)
to account for nearly 17 million deaths annually. The accurate
and timely detection of these disorders is crucial to reducing
mortality rates, but this evaluation often depends on the
knowledge and skill of the medical practitioner, leading to
potential misdiagnoses and increased medical costs [1].
In recent times, Machine Learning (ML) techniques have
been increasingly regarded as very potent predictive model
tools, especially when applied to the diagnosis of cardio-
vascular diseases [2]. Such increased emphasis on Machine
Learning is partially due to the remarkable ability of Machine
Learning to handle large and complicated datasets, uncover
hidden patterns, and obtain results often beyond the reach of
human analysis [3].
Of the wide variety of machine learning algorithms, en-
semble learning is often considered one of the most powerful
methods in the field. Ensemble learning combines several base
learners to create a combined and stronger model. As such,
ensemble learning offers many advantages that enhance its
effectiveness as a method for enhancing predictive power on
a variety of tasks [4].
Among the most extensively used ensemble algorithms
are Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting
(XGBoost). Random Forest uses a bagging method and builds
many individual decision trees, and each of these contributes
to a final classification output [5]. In contrast, XGBoost uses a
boosting approach and builds decision trees sequentially, with
each tree designed to minimize the errors of the previous ones.
This iteration helps to gradually minimize the predictive error
over many learning iterations [6].
Despite the empirically proven effectiveness of ensemble
algorithms like Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boost-
ing (XGBoost), their effectiveness is largely dependent on
the features and nature of the input data. Clinical datasets,
especially those related to cardiac diseases, tend to have high-
dimensional spaces with a very high percentage of irrelevant
or redundant features, and are prone to imbalance in general.
These issues can lead model overfitting and biased predictions
[7]. To overcome these challenges, methods like feature selec-
tion are utilized to maintain discriminating features, while data
sampling methods like the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling
Technique (SMOTE) are used to correct class imbalance and
improve model robustness [8].
This study investigates the influence of three different fea-
ture selection methods—Chi-Square, Mutual Information, and
SelectFromModel—on the performance of Random Forest and
XGBoost classifiers in terms of multiclass heart disease clas-
sification. The Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique
(SMOTE) is used to counteract the imbalance in the dataset,
and hyperparameter tuning improves prediction accuracy. In
addition, SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) is used to
explain how features contribute to the models’ predictions.

study focuses on the hematologic features remaining with clin-

II. RELATED WORKS
The field of Machine Learning (ML) has received con-
siderable interest in the medical field, particularly in the
prediction and diagnosis of cardiovascular disease. Various
research studies have indicated that ML models have the
potential to identify patterns in clinical data that often escape
human analysis. For example, Snigdha Datta et al. utilized
Logistic Regression to predict cardiovascular disease with an
accuracy of 81% [9].
Among the many algorithms studied, ensemble-based algo-
rithms like Random Forest and XGBoost have always proved
to be highly effective in cardiovascular disease prediction.
Madhumita Pal and Smita Parija reported that the Random
Forest model achieved an accuracy of more than 86.9% in
predicting heart disease [10]. Similarly, Juan Carlos Yang
found that XGBoost, when carefully tuned with appropri-
ate hyperparameters, outperformed conventional models by
achieving more than 88% accuracy and even reaching up
to 91% accuracy in certain experiments, thus proving its
credibility in solving medical datasets [11].
To further improve the precision of predictions, researchers
have used feature selection methods with the aim of reducing
dimensionality and eliminating unnecessary features. Accord-
ing to Bilal Ahmad et al., Mutual Information (MI) and Chi-
Square considerably increase the performance of classifiers
while maintaining computational efficiency [12]. Similarly,
Saikiran Kutikuppala et al. utilized the SelectFromModel fea-
ture selection method to simplify the process of classification
and maximize efficiency through the systematic selection of
the most relevant features [13].
One common problem faced in medical datasets is the
occurrence of class imbalance, which causes overfitting of the
model and biased prediction generation [7]. To overcome this
issue, numerous oversampling methods have been extensively
used, of which the most recognized one is the Synthetic Minor-
ity Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE). Anitha Juliette Albert
et al. proved that the use of SMOTE resulted in significant
improvements in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1
score compared with non-sampling datasets [14].
There has been increased emphasis on interpretability in
the field of medical machine learning in recent years. SHAP
(SHapley Additive Explanations) is a remarkable tool that
provides clear and theory-driven evaluations of feature im-
portance. According to Amira et al., SHAP ensures that the
contribution of every feature is fairly allocated, thus providing
accurate and interpretable explanations for model predictions.
Its ability to provide both global and local interpretability
makes it particularly beneficial for unraveling complex ma-
chine learning models, especially in high-stakes settings like
medical diagnosis [15].
While many studies have applied ensemble models or
feature selection separately, few have systematically examined
their combined impact on multiclass heart disease classifi-
cation. Most prior work has focused on binary classifica-
tion, leaving multiclass prediction underexplored. This study
addresses these gaps by assessing how Chi-Square, Mutual
Information, and SelectFromModel affect the performance of
Random Forest and XGBoost, supported by SMOTE and
hyperparameter tuning.

A. Dataset Description
The data used in the current study come from a medi-
cal clinic located in West Jakarta and can be accessed at
https://zenodo.org/records/15172095. The dataset in question
is obtained from a standalone cardiovascular hospital with
expertise in the diagnosis and treatment of heart diseases.
The autonomy ensures that a large percentage of patients who
report to the hospital have confirmed or suspected cardiac
conditions, thus making the dataset especially representative
and relevant to research in the prediction of heart disease.
The dataset contains ten different attributes and 4,752 in-
stances, including the class label. The label attribute, which
is represented as ”icd pr,” indicates the precise type of angina
pectoris the patient has been diagnosed with. The World Health
Organization (WHO) classifies unstable angina based on the
International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-
10), and assigns it I20.0. This class includes crescendo angina,
de novo effort angina, and provoked effort angina. In addition,
the code I20.8 covers other types of angina pectoris, which
include effort angina and stenocardia, while I20.9 is reserved
for unspecified angina pectoris, including general angina (NOS
- Not Otherwise Specified) [16]. The attributes used in this
study are described as follows:
• Gender: Categorical variable indicating patient sex (male
or female).
• Age: The patients’ ages range from 20 to 94 years.
• Erythrocyte: Red blood cells responsible for oxygen
transport. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) can
serve as a biomarker for inflammatory responses associ-
ated with cardiovascular conditions [17], [18].
• Hematocrit: Hematocrit levels are indicative of the pro-
portion of red blood cells in blood volume and can be
used to assess conditions such as anemia or polycythemia.
Higher hematocrit levels have been associated with in-
creased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) [19], [20].
• Hemoglobin: A protein in red blood cells that carries
oxygen. Low hemoglobin levels (e.g., < 14g/dL for
STEMI or < 11g/dL for NSTE-ACS) are associated with
increased cardiovascular risk [21].
• Leukocytes: White blood cell count. An elevated count
can indicate inflammation and higher cardiovascular risk
due to neutrophil and monocyte activation [22]. Leuko-
cytes are also biomarkers for infections, inflammation,
allergies, and leukemia [23].
• Thrombocyte: A high level of thrombocytes in the
patient’s blood indicates an increased risk of coronary
heart disease [24]. Any increase or decrease in the
thrombocyte level from the normal range, along with any
dysfunction, is expected to lead to complications; thus,
the determination of the level of thrombocytes can help
in the diagnosis of certain medical conditions [25].
• Hermch (MCH): Mean corpuscular hemoglobin, indicat-
ing the average amount of hemoglobin per red blood cell
[26].
• Khermchc (MCHC): Mean corpuscular hemoglobin
concentration (MCHC) measures the concentration of
hemoglobin in red blood cells [26], [27].
B. Data Preprocessing
The preprocessing phase included the conversion of cat-
egorical variables and normalization of numerical data. The
’gender’ feature was expressed in binary form, assigning
1 to females and 0 to males. Target variable ’icd pr’ was
transformed using LabelEncoder for multiclass classification.
In addition, features were standardized using the application of
StandardScaler to normalize their scales and improve learning
stability. These operations made the dataset comply with
machine learning algorithm requirements while, at the same
time, preventing the possibility of biased learning due to scale
differences among features.
C. Handling Class Imbalance
One challenge in this study was class imbalance in the target
feature that can cause overfitting and bias toward the majority
classes, thus reducing performance on minority classes [7]. In
the dataset, 44.9% of instances belong to class I20.0, 31.4% to
I20.8, with only 23.7% of it being due to I20.9, highlighting
the need for corrective measures.
To remedy the problem under study, the Synthetic Minority
Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to the dataset.
SMOTE operates by generating synthetic instances for the
minority classes through interpolation between current sam-
ples in the minority class and their k-nearest neighbors [28].
This worked to effectively balance the distribution across the
classes, with each class being approximately 33.3% of the
population in the sample.
To formalize the SMOTE approach, synthetic samples are
obtained using the following interpolation equation:

D. Feature Selection Techniques
The current study applied three different feature selection
methods—Chi-Square, Mutual Information, and SelectFrom-
Model—to analyze their performance in selecting the most
relevant features for classifying angina pectoris into different
types. Each of the main types of feature selection was catered
by a particular method: Chi-Square was used as a standard
filtering method, Mutual Information was an entropy-based
filter that is good at identifying both linear and non-linear
associations, and SelectFromModel was an embedded method
that makes use of internal model scores to determine sig-
nificance. This systematic approach allows for a comparison
between classic univariate statistical methods and model-based
approaches that are better at handling complex interactions.

First, the input features were normalized using MinMaxS-
caler to enable the use of the Chi-Square approach, since
the method requires non-negative input variables. The five
most important features were determined based on the highest
Chi-Square values. For the Mutual Information approach,
normalized data was used to determine the mutual dependence
of each feature and the target variable, again selecting the
top five features. Under the SelectFromModel approach, the
pre-trained Random Forest and XGBoost model was used to
determine feature importance scores, making it easy to select
features with importance values above the median.
E. Modeling
Random Forest and XGBoost are widely recognized as
robust models for clinical predictions [31], [32]. As methods
in ensemble learning, they naturally encompass feature selec-
tion processes that effectively counteract problems regarding
multicollinearity, noise, and irrelevant variables without requir-
ing extra preprocessing. The non-parametric nature of these
models allows detecting non-linear relationships, making them
more robust against the outliers commonly seen in clinical
data [33]. When properly tuned, these models have lower
probabilities of overfitting, even for scenarios involving class
imbalances or small sample sizes [34], making them especially
well-suited to heterogeneous and noisy data.
In the current study, Random Forest and Extreme Gradient
Boosting (XGBoost) were selected because of the proven
success of these techniques on complex classification problems
and the ability to scale to high-dimensional datasets. Random
Forest follows a bagging approach, building multiple decision
trees whose outputs are averaged to yield the final prediction
[5], while XGBoost has a boosting approach, gradually adding
trees to correct the faults of previous trees and thus systemat-
ically reducing prediction error [6].
All models were trained on the SMOTE-balanced dataset
using all feature selection variants, namely Chi-Square, Mutual
Information, and SelectFromModel, and a baseline without
feature selection. This enabled a direct comparison of their
individual impacts on performance. Hyperparameters were
optimised using grid search to ensure the optimal model
settings.
For enhancing the models’ interpretability, SHAP (SHapley
Additive Explanations) was applied on two different setups:
the baseline Random Forest model without feature selection
and the XGBoost model with Chi-Square feature selection.
The Random Forest model, which includes all the available
features, provides a holistic view of the contribution of each
clinical attribute to the predictions made. On the other hand,
the XGBoost model, which uses feature selection, provides
a more focused and understandable explanation that depends
only on the variables found to be statistically significant. This
two-pronged assessment allows for a meaningful comparison
between full and reduced feature sets and, in doing so,
highlights the attributes that are key predictors of different
types of angina pectoris.
F. Hyperparameter Tuning
In order to maximize the performance and enable a fair
comparison between the models, hyperparameter tuning was
applied on the Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers using
GridSearchCV, a module found within the Scikit-learn library.
The GridSearchCV approach is meant to find the best pa-
rameters for a classifier by tuning specific hyperparameters
which are not learned automatically when training the model,
thus improving the ability of the model to make accurate
predictions on new, never-before-seen data [29].
For the Random Forest model, the optimized hyperparam-
eters were n estimators = [50, 100, 150] and max depth =
[None, 5, 10]. For the XGBoost model, the search space
for the parameters included n estimators = [50, 100, 150]
and max depth = [3, 5, 10]. Both models used a grid
search approach with 3-fold cross-validation, with accu-
racy as the main evaluation criterion. For XGBoost, the
use label encoder=False parameter setting was used to avoid
the use of outdated features, and the ’mlogloss’ evaluation
metric was used to better support multiclass classification
problems. The best parameters found for each model were
then applied uniformly across all experimental trials.
G. Evaluation Metrics
The model’s performance was evaluated utilizing a range
of measures. Accuracy was the overall proportion of instances
correctly predicted, while precision, recall, and F1-score were
calculated for each class separately to provide a complete
assessment—a critical factor within medical datasets, where
misclassification can have severe consequences. A confusion
matrix was also generated to illustrate correct and incorrect
predictions in relation to the three classes of angina pectoris.
All the analysis steps were performed using functions from
scikit-learn.

For this analysis, strong emphasis was placed on macro-
averaged measures such as precision, recall, and F1-score to

B. Effect of SMOTE on Model Performance
To counter the class imbalance detected in the dataset,
Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was
applied before the phase where the model was trained. Table
VI contains the summary comparison between Random Forest,
XGBoost, both with and without applying SMOTE, while
omitting the consideration of feature selection.
Results clearly show that use of the SMOTE method had
resulted in considerable improvement in all the performance
parameters of both the models. For Random Forest, accuracy
improved from 45.5% to 57.1%, while the F1-score improved
from 0.44 to 0.57. Similarly, XGBoost improved its accuracy
from 48.2% to 56.4%, with corresponding improvement in the
F1-score from 0.44 to 0.56.
The improvements were especially evident in the recall
measures, which show that SMOTE strongly increases the
ability of predictive models to effectively detect instances in
minority classes. As a result, although predictive accuracy
is boosted, the fairness and robustness of the classification
system are also increased, making it extremely suitable for
use in clinical practice with imbalanced medical data.

C. Confusion Matrix Analysis
To evaluate the predictive performance for every class,
confusion matrices were created for four different settings, as
shown in Figure 2: Random Forest without feature selection,
XGBoost without feature selection, XGBoost with Chi-Square,
and XGBoost with Mutual Information. These settings include
both baseline options as well as those where feature selection
was applied to the two ensemble methods.
The Random Forest baseline produced relatively balanced
predictions across the three angina classes but with a high mis-
classification rate. XGBoost without feature selection slightly
improved identification of classes I20.8 and I20.9 but had
high false positives for class I20.0. Applying Chi-Square or
Mutual Information reduced true positive rates and increased
misclassifications in most classes, confirming that external
feature selection reduced performance compared to using the
full feature set.
Overall, the confusion matrices visually reinforce the quan-
titative findings above by illustrating class-level prediction
patterns.
D. Model Interpretability using SHAP

that defines a class of additive importance functions for fea-

Shapley values, which are rooted in cooperative game theory,
and seeks to allocate fairly the impact of each feature on the
difference between the true prediction made by the model
and the average prediction over all possible combinations of
features. The method was introduced by Lundberg and Lee
[36]. Computation of the Shapley value of a feature i is

E. Discussion and Implication
This study investigated the effect of different feature se-
lection methods on ensemble learning models for predicting
multiclass heart disease, specifically comparing configurations
of Random Forest and XGBoost both with and without feature
selection. The top-performing models did not use formal
feature selection; in particular, the standard Random Forest
yielded the highest accuracy of 57.1%, which outperformed
all variations that used Chi-Square, Mutual Information, or
SelectFromModel feature selection. The same trend was noted
with XGBoost. These results suggest that in low-dimensional
settings, the benefits derived by feature selection are minimal;

The findings indicate that tree-based ensembles like Ran-

frameworks [30].
Moreover, the SHAP analysis showed that erythrocyte
counts, hemoglobin concentrations, and hematocrit measure-
ments were always significant in model predictions. These
clinically relevant variables, linked with known cardiovascular
indexes, serve to further support correspondence between
model and existing knowledge in the domain. Importantly,
even without explicit feature selection toward known targets,
ensemble methods automatically offset these predictors. This
property reduces the need for complex preprocessing, whereas
SHAP-based interpretations provide a means for relating out-
puts to clinical expertise, enhancing explainability and building
confidence in medical decision support.
The model’s accuracy, measured at a relatively modest
57.1%, is thus limited by the dataset’s sole emphasis on
hematological markers like red blood cell counts, hemoglobin
levels, and hematocrit values. Though these markers are of
clinical interest in certain limited circumstances, they represent
only a small subset of the many variables that may impact
cardiovascular disease. Notably, the analysis did not comprise
other key risk factors, such as blood pressure measurements,
cholesterol levels, electrocardiogram findings, and disease
familial history, thus limiting the model’s ability to realize
high predictive capacity.
Despite this fact, the level of accuracy achieved should
not be ignored completely. It illustrates the possibility of
using basic and easily available blood test information for the
initial assessment of cardiovascular disease in resources-poor
settings. This study emphasizes the capability of ensemble
learning methods in deriving diagnostic information from
limited datasets, which will be a useful and easily applicable
tool for making risk assessments. The value of this study lies
less in the diagnostic accuracy of the predictors and more
in the development of a clinically meaningful, interpretable,
and practical machine learning tool within the context of
cardiovascular disease.
V. CONCLUSION
The major aim of this study was to evaluate the impact
of different feature selection methods on the performance of
ensemble machine learning models namely, Random Forest
and XGBoost in the prediction of multiclass diagnoses of
heart disease. Experimental design used a real clinical dataset
with an uneven class distribution that was counteracted by the
use of SMOTE. Various feature selection approaches, such as
Chi-Square, Mutual Information, and SelectFromModel, were
tested and compared with baseline models without the use of
feature selection.
Results demonstrated both algorithms were optimized with-
out feature selection, indicating both algorithms’ built-in fea-
ture evaluation functions were effective for identifying relevant
features. Without feature selection, Random Forest obtained
the optimal accuracy of 57.1%, while XGBoost obtained an
accuracy of 56.4%. By comparison, those models with feature
selection showed somewhat lower performance. By SHAP
analysis, erythrocyte count, hemoglobin level, and hematocrit
were identified as the key predictors for both algorithms,
paralleling already established clinical predictors for cardiac
disease.
The findings highlight the importance of evaluating whether
there is a need for external feature selection when apply-
ing tree-based ensemble methods. Additionally, the use of
interpretable models provides essential insights that enable
medical decision-making, thus increasing model transparency
and building confidence in the clinical environment. The study
is, however, limited by the inherent characteristics of the
dataset since it was obtained from a single source and included
a limited range of diagnostic groups.
Future research activities may involve investigating the
integration of a wider variety of clinical features, utilize
datasets acquired from diverse healthcare organizations, and
investigate the use of deep learning models combined with
explainability techniques. These improvements can enhance
diagnostic accuracy and the effectiveness of decision support
systems in the healthcare industry.
