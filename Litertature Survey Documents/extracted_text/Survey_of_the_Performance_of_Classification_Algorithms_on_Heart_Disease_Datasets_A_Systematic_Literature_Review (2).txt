Abstract—Machine learning is widely applied in medical 
informatics, especially for predicting heart disease. Despite 
numerous studies, there is a lack of systematic reviews that 
comprehensively map the classification algorithms used, datasets 
employed, and performance metrics evaluated within this field. 
This research provides a systematic review of the literature 
(SLR) focused on the performance of classification algorithms in 
predicting heart disease using real-world datasets. Adopting the 
PRISMA framework, we conducted a structured search across 
Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM, Springer, and ScienceDirect from 
January 2022 to December 2024. Forty-two peer-reviewed 
studies met our inclusion criteria. We extracted data on 
algorithm types, datasets, evaluation metrics, and feature 
engineering strategies. The most common datasets include 
Cleveland, Statlog, and Framingham, with Support Vector 
Machines (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) as dominant 
classifiers. However, algorithm performance varies significantly 
depending on feature selection and preprocessing techniques. 
This review identifies current research gaps, such as limited use 
of multiclass labels, insufficient real-clinical datasets, and lack of 
comparative benchmarks, suggesting directions for future 
research. 
Keywords— classification, heart disease, machine learning, 
systematic review, SVM, ensemble learning, PRISMA 
I. INTRODUCTION 
A diverse array of heart disease datasets is utilized in 
machine learning research, accessible from a variety of 
repositories, including the UCI Machine Learning Repository 
[1], Kaggle [2], PhysioNet [3], OpenML [4], and the National 
Institutes of Health (NIH) [5], among others.  
This review incorporates datasets such as Cleveland, 
Statlog (Heart), Framingham, Hungarian, Z-Alizadeh Sani, 
Long Beach VA, and South African, each offering distinct 
features and demographics.  
This paper centers on the Cleveland heart disease dataset, 
sourced from the UCI Repository, which comprises a well-
organized structure of 76 attributes and a single class label. 
Since 2022, this dataset has been referenced as secondary data 
across 28 peer-reviewed studies, underscoring its pivotal role 
in heart disease research and its extensive applicability for 
developing predictive models. 2022.  
 Machine learning represents an advanced computational 
technique capable of aiding in the classification of heart disease 
[6]. Through computational approaches, machines can learn 
from datasets, made possible by machine learning algorithms 
[7]. In machine learning, classification aims to accurately 
predict the correct label for given input data [8]. However, not 
all machine learning classification models excel in accurately 
discerning classes; a significant limitation lies in their inability 
to extrapolate beyond existing data. Nonetheless, the scope of 
application can be expanded relatively easily by integrating 
new data points [9]. This limitation in class recognition 
presents an ongoing challenge for researchers, spurring efforts 
to optimize machine learning classification methods for 
improved accuracy and adaptability. 
Optimization efforts in machine learning, particularly in 
classification, encompass various sophisticated strategies, 
including data preprocessing [10], feature selection [11], [12], 
ensemble learning [13], and deep learning techniques [14]. 
Additionally, established classification algorithms such as K-
Nearest Neighbors, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Support 
Vector Machine (SVM), and Naïve Bayes remain integral to 
the field [15]. With continuous advancements in machine 
learning and artificial intelligence, these algorithms offer 
tailored solutions for diverse, complex contexts [7].  
Classification algorithm development involves partitioning 
data into two primary sets: a larger training set, essential for 
model construction, and a test set, necessary for evaluating 
model performance and accuracy through predictive output.  
This systematic literature review (SLR) synthesizes the 
latest advancements in classification algorithm performance on 
heart disease datasets, presenting a comprehensive view of the 
state-of-the-art algorithms and setting the foundation for 
emerging research directions. By emphasizing strategies such 
as preprocessing, targeted feature selection, and ensemble 
modeling, this SLR underscores the pathways to enhance 
model 
accuracy 
and 
robustness.

II. BACKGROUND TEORY 
A. Machine Learning 
Machine learning (ML) is a key branch of artificial 
intelligence (AI) [16]–[18] Which focuses on creating 
advanced algorithms that allow computers to independently 
learn and make decisions based on data [17]. The primary aim 
of ML is to discern patterns within datasets [19], which can 
subsequently be leveraged for prediction, classification, or 
deriving insights with minimal human intervention. Challenges 
across diverse domains that prove difficult to resolve through 
traditional computational methods often become more 
manageable through using machine learning methods [20]. The 
ML process necessitates a structured learning phase utilizing 
training data. Following this, the model generated from the 
algorithm undergoes rigorous testing and validation against a 
previously unencountered test dataset. The performance of this 
model is assessed based on its accuracy, which is quantified by 
comparing the predicted outcomes against the actual values 
[21], [22]. This systematic approach not only enhances 
predictive capabilities but also contributes to the ongoing 
refinement of ML methodologies in various application. 
B. Machine Learning Classification 
Classification is a core task in machine learning [16], [17]. 
It is a key component of supervised learning techniques, 
characterized by the availability of labeled data [23]. In this 
context, the model represents trained on input features 
associated with the relevant output labels [16]. The 
foundational concept in classification begins with the number 
of classes: a binary class scenario involves the model 
classifying data into one of two possible categories, while a 
multi-class scenario requires the model to assign data to one of 
several categories. Following this, the process involves 
ensuring the availability of training data, where the model 
learns from labeled examples, and subsequently testing the 
model on previously unseen data. The last step consists of 
assessing the model's performance using standard evaluation 
metrics [16], [17], [24]. This systematic approach is essential 
for evaluating the effectiveness and precision of classification 
models across different applications. 
C. Evaluation model 
To assess the performance of machine learning 
classification models, several tools are utilized, one of which 
is the confusion matrix [17], [25]. It presents a tabular layout 
that displays the intersection of rows and columns, referred to 
as cells; each cell indicates the predicted values. This method 
assesses the performance of the classification model by 
measuring how well it aligns the predicted results compared to 
the actual outcomes. By analyzing the various components 
with analyzing the confusion matrix—containing true 
positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives—
researchers can compute valuable metrics like accuracy, 
precision, recall, and F1 score, offering a thorough assessment 
of the model's performance.

B. Review Protocol and Search Strategy 
This SLR follows the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [30]. 
Literature was retrieved from five major databases: Scopus, 
IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and ACM Digital 
Library. The search focused on articles published between 
January 2022 and December 2024. The following search 
string was used: 
("heart disease" OR "cardiac disease") AND ("classification 
algorithm" OR "machine learning") AND ("accuracy" OR 
"performance") 
C. Criteria for Inclusion and Exclusion 
To maintain the quality and relevance of the studies 
incorporated in this review, criteria for inclusion and 
exclusion were applied. Studies were included if they were 
peer-reviewed 
journal 
or 
conference 
publications, 
Concentrated on classifying heart disease through machine 
learning algorithms, presenting performance metrics such as 
accuracy, precision, and F1-score. 
Conversely, studies were excluded if they were not written 
in English, categorized as editorial notes or narrative reviews, 
or lacked sufficient methodological detail such as the 
description of the dataset, algorithm implementation, or 
evaluation process. 
D. Study Selection and Screening Process 
A total of 241 articles were initially retrieved. After 
removing duplicates (n=32), 209 papers remained for abstract 
and title screening. 83 articles were excluded during this 
screening due to irrelevance to the research focus, such as not 
addressing classification algorithms or heart disease. 
Of the remaining 126 full-text articles assessed for 
eligibility, 84 were excluded for the following reasons, 32 
articles lacked sufficient methodological detail, 27 did not 
report quantitative performance metrics, 15 were general 
reviews not focused on classification algorithms, 10 were non-
English or had inaccessible full texts. 
Finally, 42 articles satisfied the inclusion criteria and were 
incorporated into the final synthesis. The Fig. 2 illustrates the 
detailed process of study identification, screening, exclusion, 
and inclusion based on the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [30]. It 
includes reasons and counts for exclusions at each stage to 
improve transparency and reproducibility.

E. Quality Assessment 
To evaluate the methodological rigor of the included 
studies, a structured quality assessment was conducted based 
on four criteria: (1) use of a valid and publicly recognized 
dataset; (2) clear definition and explanation of algorithm and 
methodological procedures; (3) provision of performance 
evaluation metrics such as accuracy or F1-score; and (4) 
sufficient documentation to ensure reproducibility of results. 
Each study was scored on a binary scale (0 or 1) for each 
criterion, resulting in a total quality score ranging from 0 to 4. 
Only studies with a minimum score of 3 were considered 
eligible for inclusion in this review to ensure robustness and 
reliability of the synthesized findings 
F. Data Extraction 
A standardized approach to data extraction was 
implemented to ensure uniformity and comprehensiveness. 
For each selected study, the following elements were 
systematically recorded: the names of the authors, year of 
publication, dataset(s) used, feature selection techniques 
applied, classification algorithm(s) employed, presented 
performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and 
F1-score, and any noted limitations or unique contributions. 
These extracted attributes formed the basis for the synthesis of 
results and the comparative analysis presented in Section IV

B. Feature Selection and Dataset Attributes (RQ2) 
Although the Cleveland dataset contains 76 features, most 
studies use only 13–14 key attributes. Typical features consist 
of age, sex, chest pain type, resting blood pressure, serum 
cholesterol, and maximum heart rate. Techniques for feature 
selection, including Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), 
Genetic Algorithms (GA), and Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA), are employed to reduce dimensionality and improve 
model performance. Studies that applied feature selection 
methods generally reported increased accuracy, often by 2–
10%, compared to models that utilize the complete set of 
features. For example, models that implemented RFE on the 
Cleveland dataset achieved accuracy improvements from 
~85% to 95%. This suggests that selecting relevant and non-
redundant attributes significantly contributes to model 
generalizability and computational efficiency. 
Notably, a study by Komal Kumar et al. [35] implemented 
RFE in combination with SVM and reported an accuracy of 
89.91%, outperforming configurations without feature 
selection. Similarly, Chandrasekhar and Peddakrishna [34] 
combined Relief F-based selection with ensemble classifiers 
and achieved up to 95% accuracy. These examples illustrate 
the importance of carefully engineered feature subsets in 
maximizing model performance while reducing computational 
costs.

C. Algorithm Performance (RQ3) 
SVM (n=15) and RF (n=13) are the most frequently used 
classifiers, followed by Logistic Regression, KNN, and Naïve 
Bayes. Ensemble techniques like Gradient Boosting, 
XGBoost, and AdaBoost demonstrated improved accuracy 
when paired with feature selection. However, CNNs and Deep 
Neural Networks are underexplored due to dataset size 
limitations. 
In terms of performance, SVM classifiers achieved 
accuracies ranging between 85% and 95% across various 
studies. For instance, Ullah et al. [71] reported an SVM 
accuracy of 97.9% using the Cleveland dataset. Random 
Forest classifiers also demonstrated strong performance, with 
accuracies typically falling between 88% and 95%, such as in 
the work of Chandrasekhar and Peddakrishna [34]. Logistic 
Regression models showed slightly lower accuracy, generally 
ranging from 80% to 89%, while KNN and Naïve Bayes 
varied between 75% and 90% depending on the dataset and 
preprocessing applied. 
Boosting algorithms reported some of the highest 
accuracies when combined with optimized feature selection. 
Dalal et al. [72] achieved 99.1% accuracy using a combination 
of QUEST, Random Forest, and Neural Networks. Similarly, 
the use of XGBoost and Gradient Boosting in studies by Bora 
et al. [73] and Budholiya et al. [44] resulted in accuracies 
between 95% and 98%.

D. Trends, Gaps, and Challenges (RQ4) 
The examination of the chosen studies highlights various 
trends, challenges, and research gaps in the field of heart 
disease classification through machine learning. One 
prominent trend is the dominant reliance on open-access 
datasets such as Cleveland and Statlog. These datasets are 
widely used due to their accessibility and structured formats, 
enabling comparative evaluations across different studies. In 
terms of classification tasks, most research is focused on 
binary classification (i.e., forecasting the presence or absence 
of heart disease), with limited exploration of multiclass 
classification that could reflect various stages or types of heart 
conditions. Furthermore, the emergence of ensemble learning 
and hybrid modeling techniques has gained traction, 
particularly in achieving improved accuracy and robustness. 
Despite these advancements, several gaps persist. Many 
studies still depend on benchmark datasets rather than real 
clinical data sourced directly from hospitals or health 
institutions, limiting the generalizability of their findings. 
Moreover, the interpretability of machine learning models is 
still insufficiently explored, with limited studies utilizing 
explainable AI (XAI) methods to enhance the transparency 
and 
comprehensibility 
of 
predictions 
for 
medical 
professionals. 
Major obstacles also impede further advancement, such as 
the problem of imbalanced class distributions in datasets, 
which can bias classifiers toward the majority class, and the 
absence of standardized benchmarks that complicate 
performance 
comparisons.

practices, which affects reproducibility across studies.

V. CONCLUSION 
This systematic review offers a thorough synthesis of 
recent studies on the effectiveness of classification algorithms 
used on heart disease datasets. The Cleveland dataset became 
the most commonly used source, primarily due to its structured 
features, moderate size, and established benchmarking 
potential. Feature selection techniques such as RFE, PCA, and 
GA were shown to enhance classification accuracy by up to 
10%, with studies demonstrating substantial improvements 
when these methods were used. 
Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) 
remain the most frequently implemented algorithms, offering 
robust performance with accuracies ranging between 85–
97.9% and 88–95%, respectively. Ensemble and boosting 
methods such as XGBoost and hybrid approaches showed 
superior accuracy, with some reaching 99.1% when combined 
with optimized feature engineering. 
Emerging trends include an increased focus on ensemble 
learning, while challenges persist around class imbalance, lack 
of real clinical datasets, and the limited use of explainable AI 
techniques. This review highlights critical research gaps such 
as the need for multiclass classification studies, model 
transparency, and standardized benchmarking frameworks. 
Future work should prioritize expanding dataset diversity, 
utilizing models that are interpretable and explainable, 
assessing models in multiclass scenarios or real-time clinical 
environments. Such advancements are essential to enhanced 
the reliability, generalizability, and the clinical applicability of 
machine learning-based heart disease prediction systems.
